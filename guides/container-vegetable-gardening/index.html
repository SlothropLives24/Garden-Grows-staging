Container vegetable gardening for beginners

You can grow real food in pots on a patio, balcony, or step - here's how to pick the pot, what to fill it with, what to grow, and the one habit that decides whether it lives.

What you need

  • Pots with drainage holes - bigger than you think, and at least one per crop
  • A bag of potting mix (not garden soil - here's why)
  • A spot with six or more hours of sun a day
  • A daily habit of checking the soil in summer

A container garden is the lowest-commitment way to grow food. A few pots on a patio, a balcony, a front step, or a rented yard you can't dig up will grow herbs, salad, peppers, and more, and you can move them to chase the sun or dodge a late frost. If you're still weighing pots against a bed in the ground, the garden types guide compares them. This one assumes you've picked pots and answers the four questions that decide whether they work: what pot, what soil, what to grow, and how to keep them alive.

Pick a bigger pot than you think you need

Almost every beginner's container is too small. A bigger pot holds more soil, and more soil is a bigger water reservoir and more root room - both of which make the pot far more forgiving. Rough floors that work: a large leafy herb or a pepper wants at least a 20–25 cm8–10 inch pot; a tomato or anything that sprawls wants a 40 cm15 inch pot or a five-gallon bucket. The only non-negotiable is a drainage hole - a pot without one drowns roots in the first heavy watering. Material matters at the margins: plastic and glazed ceramic hold moisture longer than unglazed terracotta, which wicks water out through its sides and dries fastest of all.

The one demand a pot makes: water

This is the rule that actually kills container plants, and it's the reason all the pot-size advice above matters:

Well established

A container dries out faster than the same plant in the ground, and it is the one place where a day's inattention can cost you the plant.

suboptimal if ignored → advise

Mechanism: A container holds a small fixed volume of soil, its whole root zone sits above ground rather than connected to the soil below, and a porous pot loses water from its sides as well as its surface. The reservoir is smaller and it empties faster.

Evidence: confirmed against 3 sources in the research-based literature.

remedy

Check a container at least once a day, and twice when it is hot, dry or windy - in high summer it may genuinely need water more than once a day. A plastic pot holds moisture longer than an unglazed clay one, and a bigger pot longer than a small one.

A pot's whole root zone sits above ground in a small, fixed volume of soil, cut off from the moisture of the earth below. It empties fast. In high summer a small pot in the wind can need water more than once a day. So the habit that keeps a container garden alive is simple: check it every day, twice when it's hot - push a finger into the soil, and water when the top feels dry. The engine will not hand you a watering schedule, because how much depends on the weather, the pot, and the day; what it tells you is to look, more often than you'd look at the ground.

Fill it with potting mix, not garden soil

Reach for a bag of potting mix, not a shovel of soil from the yard. Garden soil packs down hard in the closed space of a pot, holds too much water, and drains poorly - the opposite of what roots in a container need. Potting mixes are built light and open so they hold moisture but still drain. Because a pot is a closed system and frequent watering washes nutrients out the bottom, container plants also need feeding more regularly than the same plant in open ground - follow the rate on whatever fertilizer you use. (For a plant in the ground the honest first step is a soil test; a bag of potting mix has no such history to read, so a balanced feed at the label rate is the standard.)

Don't bother with gravel in the bottom

A common instruction is to put a layer of gravel or broken pot shards in the bottom "for drainage." The honest status of that advice is: it doesn't do what it's sold to do, in either direction.

Contested

A layer of gravel or pot shards at the bottom of a container improves drainage.

The old "never put gravel in the bottom of a pot" rule turns out to be overstated — a recent controlled test found a coarse bottom layer usually helps drainage a little or does nothing, not that it hurts. It's also no cure: a good potting mix and a real drainage hole matter far more. Layer or don't; just don't count on it to fix a soggy mix.

what is actually known

This one is genuinely unsettled, and the popular "it's a myth, gravel makes drainage worse" answer overstates the case. The theory behind that answer is the perched water table: water is held in the fine mix by capillarity and does not cross freely into a coarse layer, so a gravel bottom was predicted to push the saturated zone up into the root ball. But a 2025 controlled experiment on real containers found the opposite of the prediction — a coarse bottom layer almost always reduced or did not change water retention, and essentially never increased it, with a coarse-sand layer draining best. So a drainage layer does not reliably harm drainage and may modestly help. It is also not the fix it is sometimes sold as: a well-drained potting mix and an open drainage hole matter far more than anything layered at the bottom.

what this rests on - one pointer

PLoS ONE (2025), 'Effect of drainage layers on water retention of potting media in containers' (read 2026-07-14 via PMC PMC11856292): 'Drainage layers were almost universally found to either decrease or have no effect on the water-holding capacity of containers'; 'any drainage layer was likely to reduce water retention of any medium, and almost never increased it' — a controlled result that contradicts the perched-water-table prediction that a coarse bottom layer worsens drainage. Grade demoted F -> D 2026-07-14 on this evidence.

The thing that actually governs drainage is the mix and the hole, not a layer at the bottom. Fill the whole pot with good mix, make sure the hole is open, and skip the gravel - or add it if you like, just don't count on it to rescue a soggy pot.

What grows well in a pot

Favor the compact and the fast over the sprawling and the huge. Reliable in containers: leaf lettuce and other salad greens, most herbs, bush beans, radishes, chard, and peppers - a pepper is the classic patio crop, productive in a modest pot. Determinate ("bush") or dwarf cultivars of tomato and squash are bred for exactly this and beat their full-size cousins in a pot. The sprawlers - full-size indeterminate tomatoes, winter squash, sweet corn - can be done, but only in the biggest containers, and corn in particular needs a block to pollinate that a few pots can't give it. Start with the easy ones. When you're ready to put a bed in the ground, the first bed walkthrough counts the timing from your frost dates.
