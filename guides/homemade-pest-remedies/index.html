Which homemade pest and weed remedies work

Dish soap, vinegar, eggshells and human hair are the free kitchen-cupboard fixes. The one most often sold as the safe choice is the one that damages your plants.

What you need

  • A clear idea of which pest you have. Most of these fail by being aimed at the wrong thing.
  • For slugs, a torch and ten minutes after dark
  • For deer, a tape measure, because the answer is height

The kitchen-cupboard remedies are the most-shared advice in gardening, and they carry an extra promise on top of “this works”: this is the gentle option. They’re household things, so they must be mild, free, and already under the sink.

That framing is the problem. Household things can be harsh, and the remedy most often recommended as the safe alternative is the one that damages your plants.

These have the same shape as the soil myths: a true fact at the front, a plausible jump, and no measurement. As there, each card below says how sure the evidence is. A person has checked all four of these cards against their sources.

Does a dish-soap spray work?

Dish soap makes a safe, effective homemade insecticide, the same as insecticidal soap.

Refuted

Dish soap isn't the same as insecticidal soap. Detergents strip the leaf's protective wax and can damage foliage, so the "safe homemade spray" idea backfires. If you want a soap spray, use a real insecticidal soap; it works on contact against soft-bodied pests like aphids.

What is known

Dish detergents are not insecticidal soaps. Insecticidal soaps are potassium salts of fatty acids made for use on plants; detergents are degreasers that can damage plants by dissolving the waxy cuticle that protects the leaf, so the "safe homemade version" framing is exactly backwards. If you want a soap spray, use a labeled insecticidal soap, which works on contact against soft-bodied pests such as aphids.

The source

homegarden.cahnr.uconn.edu Read by a person on September 24, 2026

Insecticidal Soaps University of Connecticut Home & Garden Education Center
Dishwashing soaps and detergents are designed to remove grease from dishes and may cause plant damage by dissolving the waxy cuticle on plant leaf surfaces

Start here, because the popular framing of this one is exactly backwards.

The advice is usually offered as the safe, homemade version of a commercial product: skip the insecticidal soap and use a squirt of washing-up liquid. Dish detergents aren’t mild insecticidal soaps, though. Detergents are degreasers. That’s their whole job in your sink, and on a leaf what they dissolve is the waxy coat that protects it. Repeated or strong sprays cause a chemical burn, worst in heat and sun, which is exactly when people reach for it.

The myth survives because it does kill things. A soap spray kills soft-bodied insects like aphids and mites on contact, so you see a result and figure it worked. What you don’t see in the same moment is the damage to the leaf.

Real insecticidal soaps are specific potassium-salt fatty acids, made to kill the insect without stripping the plant. If you want a soap spray, buy that product. It isn’t expensive, and the “homemade equivalent” was never equivalent.

What about a vinegar spray?

Household vinegar is an effective weed killer.

Contested

Vinegar only burns the parts of a weed it touches, so it works on tiny seedlings, but bigger and perennial weeds grow back from the roots. It also harms any plant it hits, crops included. The strong "horticultural" kinds can burn skin and eyes. Use it for seedlings in paving cracks. It isn't a garden weed killer.

What is known

Household vinegar is about 5% acetic acid, a contact herbicide. It burns only the parts of a plant it touches, fastest in bright sun, and gives excellent control of very small annual broadleaf weeds. It does not move to the roots, so larger and perennial weeds can regrow a week or so later, and it is non-selective, injuring any plant it lands on. Stronger products are more dangerous to the person using them. At 11% acetic acid or more, vinegar can burn skin and cause severe eye injury, and 20-30% formulations call for protective gear. It is most useful for weeds in gravel and on patios and sidewalks. It is not a general weed killer.

The 2 sources

extension.umd.edu Read by a person on September 24, 2026

Household vinegar, about 5% acetic acid, is a contact burn. Sprayed on a sunny day it scorches the top growth of young, small annual weeds. That much is real, and if you’ve got seedlings coming up through pavement cracks it’s a perfectly reasonable thing to use.

Two limits make it useless as a general weedkiller. It doesn’t move into the roots, so anything established, and anything perennial, regrows from the crown within a week or two. You get a satisfying brown-off followed by the same weed. It also can’t pick and choose. It burns whatever it lands on, your crops included, which makes it a real hazard in a bed, where a path would be fine.

The upgrade has a trap of its own. The stronger “horticultural” vinegar sold at 20% and up is caustic, so it’s no gentle stand-in for herbicide, and it needs handling that way. The word “vinegar” sounds reassuring, and the concentration doesn’t back that up.

Do eggshells stop slugs?

Crushed eggshells deter slugs.

Contested

Crushed eggshells haven't been shown to deter slugs. Use beer traps, hand-pick at night, or put down iron-phosphate bait, which is safer around pets and wildlife. Put the shells in the compost for the organic matter. Their calcium releases too slowly to matter.

What is known

The claim is that sharp shell edges form a barrier slugs won't cross, but crushed eggshells have not been shown to deter slugs. Nor are the shells much use for their calcium, because they break down too slowly to supply it, and many soils already have plenty. Their real value is as organic matter in the compost. For slugs, what works is trapping, hand-picking at night and iron- phosphate bait, and bait alone will not control them in the long term.

The claim is specific and testable: sharp edges make a barrier a slug won’t cross.

Slugs glide on a layer of mucus, and they cross truly sharp surfaces without any trouble. This is an animal that can travel along a razor blade. Crushed eggshell isn’t even in that category for long, because it weathers to blunt fragments quickly. The barrier isn’t sharp enough to begin with, and soon it isn’t sharp at all.

What does work on slugs is unglamorous: beer traps, hand-picking after dark (it feels like drudgery and is startlingly effective, because that’s when they’re out), and iron-phosphate bait where the pressure’s heavy.

The eggshells still have a use. As they break down they add a little calcium, so put them in the compost, which is where their real contribution was all along. The same trap shows up with blossom-end rot. Eggshells are the folk answer to that too, and they fail there for the same reason they fail here.

Do hair, soap, or urine keep animals away?

Sprinkling human hair, soap shavings, or urine around the garden reliably keeps deer and rabbits out.

Contested

Hair, soap and other home-remedy scents aren't a reliable way to keep deer or rabbits out. They need renewing, the animals get used to them, and deer have even eaten the soap. A fence is the dependable fix, and for deer it has to be tall. Store-bought repellents do better than home remedies, but most can't go on food crops.

What is known

Hair, soap and similar home remedies are unreliable. Extension services call them questionable at best, deer have been seen eating the soap, and what works in one garden can fail where deer are more numerous. Hair and soap bags have to be renewed monthly, and deer and rabbits get used to repellent smells. A fence is what dependably keeps them out. For deer, the conventional deer- proof fence is 8 feet of woven wire, and for rabbits fencing is the most effective protection. Commercial repellents are more reliable than home remedies, but most cannot be used on edible crops and their effect is temporary.

Scent-based deterrents fail differently from the others. They’re aimed at a real mechanism, since smell does affect browsing, and they often work for a few days, which is just long enough to convince the gardener.

Then two things happen. The scent weathers away, and the animal gets used to it. A hungry deer learns that nothing bad follows the smell, and comes back. That’s why these remedies are so inconsistent between gardens and between seasons, and why everyone knows someone they “worked” for.

If deer or rabbits are doing real damage, the dependable answer is a physical barrier of the right height, which for deer means properly tall. Rotating a commercial repellent helps. The rotation matters, because getting used to it is the failure mode. Choosing resistant plants helps. Hair clippings in a stocking don’t.

Physical exclusion comes up again with insects. It’s why floating row covers work on insect pests when sprays disappoint. A barrier the animal can’t get past doesn’t depend on the animal’s opinion.

How do I tell a real remedy from a useless one?

These four break in three distinct ways, and the ways they fail are more useful to know than the four facts:

  • It backfires - the remedy harms the plant, and the visible kill hides the invisible damage (dish soap).
  • It does less than the claim - real but partial, so it works on the easy case and gets stretched to the hard one (vinegar on seedlings, sold as a weedkiller).
  • The animal adapts - a real short-term effect that fades, which is the hardest to disbelieve because everyone’s seen it work once (hair, soap, urine).

None of these are stupid ideas. Each started with something true. What none of them had was anyone checking whether the effect held up against an established weed, a mucus-gliding slug, or a hungry deer in August.
