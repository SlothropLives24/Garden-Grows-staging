Rhubarb - the stalks are food, the leaves are poison

Rhubarb is one of the easiest perennials you can plant, and the only common garden vegetable where part of what you harvest is genuinely toxic. Which part, why, and the two things about it that get repeated online without evidence.

What you need

  • A permanent spot you will not dig - this plant stays for a decade or more
  • Full sun
  • A knife, and the habit of cutting the leaf off in the garden rather than in the kitchen

In this guide

  • What's the one rule I can't ignore with rhubarb?

Rhubarb is close to the easiest thing in this whole library. You plant a crown once, you keep the grass off it, and it comes back every spring for a decade or more without being asked. It is one of the few crops that genuinely rewards neglect.

It is also the only common garden vegetable where you handle a poisonous part of the plant on every single harvest, and where the ordinary way of carrying it into the kitchen is to bring that part with you.

The one rule that matters

Well established

Rhubarb leaf blades are poisonous and must never be eaten - only the leafstalks (the petioles) are edible. Harvest the stalks and cut the leaves off.

suboptimal if ignored → advise

Mechanism: The leaf blades hold a toxic concentration of oxalic acid - and, per NDSU, anthraquinone glycosides - which the edible petioles carry only in trace amounts. Oxalic acid binds calcium and can damage kidney function; eating the blades causes cramping, nausea and, in quantity, can be fatal. The stalks are safe because the toxins concentrate in the leaf, not the petiole.

Evidence: confirmed against 5 sources in the research-based literature.

the 5 sources

UW-Madison / Wisconsin Master Gardener (Susan Mahr), 'Rhubarb, Rheum rhabarbarum', mastergardener.extension.wisc.edu/files/2018/04/rhubarb.pdf (read 2026-08-05): 'The leaves, however, contain a toxic amount of oxalic acid and are poisonous. The ensuing cramps, nausea and sometimes death from ingestion suppressed interest in the plant for about two hundred years.'

NDSU Extension, 'Dakota Gardener: Is Frost-Damaged Rhubarb Toxic?', ag.ndsu.edu/news/columns/dakota-gardener/dakota-gardener-is-frost-damaged-rhubarb-toxic (read 2026-08-05), names BOTH compounds and the edible/toxic split outright: 'The leaves contain oxalic acid which can damage kidney function'; 'recent scientific studies show the presence of other compounds such as anthraquinone glycosides that may contribute to the toxicity of the leaves'; 'Rhubarb stems (petioles) which resemble celery stalks are the edible portion of the plant because they contain only trace amounts of toxic compounds.' The SAME page is why the frost->toxin belief is excluded: 'A hard freeze is widely believed to cause the movement of toxic compounds from the leaves into the stems' ... 'I could not find any scientific studies to confirm or disprove this belief.'

U. of Illinois Extension (Good Growing), 'To rhubarb or not to rhubarb?', extension.illinois.edu/blogs/good-growing/2023-06-08-rhubarb-or-not-rhubarb (read 2026-08-05): 'Do not consume leaf blades, they contain large amounts of oxalic acid which can limit the body's ability to take up calcium.'

UMN Extension, 'Growing rhubarb in home gardens', extension.umn.edu/vegetables/growing-rhubarb (read 2026-08-05): 'Rhubarb leaves are toxic. Do not eat them.'

NC State Extension Gardener Plant Toolbox, 'Rheum rhabarbarum', plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/rheum-rhabarbarum/ (read 2026-08-05): Poison Severity 'Medium'; Poison Parts 'Leaves'; Toxic Principle 'Anthraquinone glycosides and soluble oxalates'; Poison Symptoms 'TOXIC ONLY IF LARGE QUANTITIES EATEN. Abdominal cramps, burning of mouth and throat, headache, weakness, nausea and vomiting, coma; may be fatal'.

remedy

Cut the leaf blades off and discard them; eat only the stalks. Never cook or eat the leaves.

The split is clean and it is the same in all five extension sources behind that card. The leaf blade - the big crinkled sheet at the top - holds a toxic concentration of oxalic acid, and, according to NDSU, anthraquinone glycosides alongside it. The petiole - the stalk you actually eat, the thing that looks like pink celery - carries only trace amounts of the same compounds. This is not a case where one part is riskier than another; it is a case where one part is food and the other is not.

Oxalic acid binds calcium, and in quantity it can damage kidney function. What poisoning looks like, per NC State's toolbox, is abdominal cramps, burning of the mouth and throat, headache, weakness, nausea and vomiting - and in enough quantity it can be fatal. That toolbox rates the severity medium, and specifies it is toxic only if large quantities are eaten.

So: not a plant to be frightened of. A plant to be tidy about.

The habit worth building

Cut the leaf off in the garden, not in the kitchen. It takes a second, it means the toxic part never enters the house, and it removes the entire category of accident - a leaf left on the counter beside the stalks, a helpful person adding "the green bit" to the pot, a child who has just learned that rhubarb is food.

You will also see the advice to pull rhubarb rather than cut it, twisting the stalk away from the crown. That is about the plant's health rather than yours, and it is compatible with the above: pull the stalk, then take the leaf off where it fell.

Two things you will read that this guide will not repeat

Both of these are in the corpus's record for this rule as deliberate exclusions - claims that were looked at and left out, which is different from claims nobody considered.

"A hard freeze moves the toxins from the leaves into the stalks." This is stated widely, including by one extension service. NDSU went looking for the studies and reported that it could find none confirming or disproving it. So the honest position is that nobody has shown this either way, and this site does not carry a contested claim as though it were settled. If your rhubarb has been through a hard freeze, the sources do not support telling you the stalks are now dangerous - and they equally do not support telling you they are definitely fine.

"Rhubarb leaves are toxic to livestock." Asserted in plenty of places, but the corpus found it only in non-extension secondary sources, so it waits for a source of the same grade as the five behind the main claim. Absence here means "not established to this site's standard", not "false".

There is a third question this guide simply cannot answer: whether the leaves are safe to compost. Nothing in the corpus grades it, no source behind this rule addresses it, and inventing a confident answer is exactly what the rest of this library exists to avoid.

Where to put it, and why that decision is harder to undo than it looks

Rhubarb is a perennial, and perennials have one siting rule that outranks everything else:

Well established

Perennial members must not be sited in ground that is annually turned or rotated.

fatal if ignored → block

Mechanism: Tillage destroys the crown. Rotation moves the annuals; the perennial cannot move.

Evidence: settled without a trial - mechanics.

remedy

Split perennial members into a permanent border; rotate the annuals around it.

Read the full rule → · open in the planner ↗

A rhubarb crown put into a bed you rotate gets destroyed by the first season's digging. It needs ground you have decided not to disturb - a permanent border, a corner, the end of a bed you will work around rather than through. Full sun, and give it more room than the first-year plant seems to need, because it will fill it.

This is the same decision the fruit tree guild guide opens with, and for the same reason: a rotation works by moving crops, a perennial works by staying put, and the two cannot share ground.

The short version

Plant it once, somewhere you will never dig. Harvest the stalks. Cut the leaves off before you leave the garden and put them on the ground, not in the basket. And when the internet tells you something confident about frost and toxins, notice that the extension service that went looking could not find a study either way.
