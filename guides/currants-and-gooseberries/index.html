Currants and gooseberries - check your state before you plant

These are among the few fruits that crop well in part shade, and they are also the only garden fruit with a legal history. A federal ban, lifted in 1966, and a handful of states that still restrict planting - here is what the restriction is actually protecting, and how to plant around it.

What you need

  • Your state's current rules on Ribes - this is the one crop where that is step one
  • A permanent spot; these are perennial shrubs, not an annual bed
  • A note of whether there are white pines nearby

In this guide

  • Is there really a law about growing currants?

Most of this library is about biology. This guide is about biology and law, because currants and gooseberries are the one thing you might plant in a vegetable garden where the first question is not "will it grow here" but "am I allowed."

They are worth the trouble. Ribes are among the very few fruits that crop properly in part sun, which makes them one of the only real answers to "what can I grow along that shady fence." They are shallow-rooted, self-fertile - so one bush fruits on its own - and they are hardy well north of where most fruit gives up.

And they spent most of the twentieth century illegal.

The rule, and what it is actually protecting

Well established

Currants and gooseberries (Ribes) are an alternate host of white pine blister rust, and some states regulate or ban planting them - especially black currant. Check your state's rules before planting, and where white pines are nearby choose a rust-resistant cultivar.

suboptimal if ignored → advise

Mechanism: White pine blister rust (Cronartium ribicola) needs BOTH a Ribes and a five-needle (white) pine to complete its life cycle. The rust is a minor nuisance on Ribes but can be lethal to white pines, which is why some states restrict Ribes planting - strictest for black currant (Ribes nigrum), the most susceptible type. A rust-immune cultivar breaks the cycle: it cannot host the rust, so it cannot pass it to a pine.

Evidence: confirmed against 5 sources in the research-based literature.

remedy

Check your state's Ribes regulations before planting - a 1900s federal ban was lifted in 1966, but some states still restrict planting, especially of black currant. Where white pines grow nearby, plant a rust-immune cultivar - 'Consort', 'Crusader' or 'Titania' black currant, or a resistant gooseberry - which cannot carry the rust to the pine.

Read the mechanism carefully, because the shape of it explains everything else. White pine blister rust needs two hosts to complete its life cycle: a Ribes and a five-needle white pine. Take either one away and the fungus cannot finish.

Now the part that surprises people: the rust is barely a problem for your currants. UMN calls it little more than a nuisance to the grower. It is the white pines it kills. So the restriction was never about protecting the fruit crop or protecting you - it is about protecting a timber tree from a disease your shrub can carry to it. That is why this is one of the few pieces of gardening advice that is genuinely about your neighbours rather than your garden.

The federal ban went in during the early 1900s and was lifted in 1966. Some states still restrict planting, most often black currant specifically.

One honest note about this particular card

That card carries a line the others in this library do not: its evidence is not yet confirmed against its sources. Five extension services are quoted on it and they agree with each other, but a human has not yet re-opened those pages to verify the quotations, so the site says so on the card rather than quietly presenting it as settled.

It happens not to change what you should do. The advice this rule gives is go and check your own state's current rules - and that is the right move regardless of how well-sourced the rule is, because regulations change and a citation from last year cannot tell you what your state says today.

What to actually do

Check your state first, and check it yourself. This site deliberately does not publish a list of which states restrict Ribes. That is not laziness - it is that a state list rots. Regulations get amended, some states have partial rules by county, and several New England states that once restricted now say they have no regulations at present. A list published here would be wrong eventually and confidently wrong in the meantime. Your state's department of agriculture is the source that is current.

Know which one you are buying. Susceptibility is not uniform across the genus:

  • Black currant (Ribes nigrum) is the most susceptible, and the one restrictions target most often.
  • Red currants are far less so - USU notes most red currant varieties are actually immune.
  • Gooseberries sit in between, with resistant varieties available.

If white pines grow nearby, plant a resistant cultivar. This is the move that resolves the whole problem, because it breaks the cycle at your end: a rust-immune plant cannot host the rust, so it cannot pass it to a pine. The cultivars named across those extension sources are 'Consort', 'Crusader' and 'Titania' for black currant, with resistant gooseberries available too.

Siting, which is the same story as every perennial here

Well established

Perennial members must not be sited in ground that is annually turned or rotated.

fatal if ignored → block

Mechanism: Tillage destroys the crown. Rotation moves the annuals; the perennial cannot move.

Evidence: settled without a trial - mechanics.

remedy

Split perennial members into a permanent border; rotate the annuals around it.

Read the full rule → · open in the planner ↗

A currant bush is a permanent shrub. It goes in ground you have decided not to turn, and the annuals rotate around it - the same decision the fruit tree guild guide opens with, for the same reason.

The part-shade tolerance is the practical gift here. Most fruit demands full sun and sulks without it; Ribes will crop along a wall, under the edge of a canopy, or on the north side of something, which is usually ground a vegetable garden has written off.

The short version

Check your state, then buy accordingly: red currants and gooseberries are the low-risk end, black currant is the one restrictions name, and a rust-immune cultivar solves it where white pines are close. Then treat it as what it is - a permanent shrub in permanent ground, quietly producing fruit in the shady corner where nothing else would.
