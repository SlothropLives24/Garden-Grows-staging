Weeds - what actually works, and the folklore that doesn't

Two habits beat everything: never let a weed set seed, and keep the soil covered. The mulch depths, the shallow hoe, the vinegar myth, and the weeds you plant yourself.

What you need

  • A sharp hoe, used shallow, and ten minutes a week while weeds are tiny
  • Mulch - straw, leaves, wood chips, or a living cover
  • Ruthlessness about flowers - a weed that sets seed wins the next five years

Most weed control is two habits: never let a weed set seed, and keep the soil covered so the seeds already there can't start. Everything else - the hoe technique, the mulch depths, the folklore sprays - hangs off those two, and none of it replaces them.

Two enemies, two strategies

Weeds split into two camps that want opposite treatment, and misreading which one you're fighting wastes the season.

Annuals - lamb's quarters, purslane, crabgrass and their crowd - live entirely by seed, and every garden soil already holds a deep bank of it, banked by years of exactly one weed somewhere being allowed to flower. You can't empty the bank; you can stop making deposits (nothing flowers, ever) and stop making withdrawals easy (cover the surface, disturb the soil shallowly so buried seed stays buried and dark).

Perennials - quackgrass, bindweed, Canada thistle - live by their roots, and a mulch laid over a living perennial root system is a blanket over a spring: they come through. The extension guidance is blunt that unless the perennials are genuinely gone, they will keep emerging in a new bed - so dig them out first, whole root systems and all, and treat every regrown shoot as a withdrawal from the root's reserves. Persistence wins this one; there is no clever shortcut.

Mulch: the standing defence

Mulch works by the least glamorous mechanism in gardening: it blocks light at the soil surface, so weed seeds can't get started and small seedlings smother. The depths matter, and the extension figures are specific:

  • Wood chips, shredded leaves and most organic mulches: about 8 cm3 inches.
  • Straw and hay: deeper, 10-15 cm4 to 6 inches, because they pack down.
  • Beside low crops like lettuce and radishes: a lighter 4-5 cm1½ to 2 inches so the mulch doesn't swallow the row.

Three cautions ride along. Hay carries its own weed seed, so straw beats hay where you have the choice. Don't mulch with clippings from a recently herbicide-treated lawn - the same carryover warning the compost guide explains in full. And lay organic mulch after the soil has warmed in early summer, not onto cold spring ground, or it holds the chill against the crops it's meant to help.

There's also a mulch you plant, and it's the one weed tool in the corpus that carries a grade:

A good hunch

Living mulch suppresses weeds and conserves soil moisture.

suboptimal if ignored → advise

Mechanism: A living cover keeps a leaf canopy over the ground: it intercepts light at the soil surface, so weed seedlings are shaded out before they establish, and it slows surface evaporation, so the soil holds more water. In a Three Sisters planting this is the squash's role — its broad leaves shade the bed.

A low living cover - clover under taller crops, or a cover crop through the off-season - does the same light-blocking job while it feeds the ground.

The hoe, used shallow and early

The hoe beats the hand for annual weeds, on two conditions the extension guidance is careful about. Use it shallow - a light scrape that cuts seedlings at the surface, because chopping deep damages your vegetables' roots and lifts a fresh load of buried weed seed into the light. And use it early, while weeds are at the white-thread stage, too small to pull by hand and easiest to kill. Ten unhurried minutes a week in that window replaces an August of misery.

Hand-pulling keeps its place for the perennials (take the root, not the top) and for anything grown big beside a crop stem where the hoe can't go. And the one rule with no exceptions, worth repeating because it's the whole seed-bank strategy: nothing flowers. A weed you haven't time to pull today can still lose its flower head in passing.

The folklore, checked

Weed folklore follows the same pattern as the rest of the folklore shelf - a narrow, real effect inflated into a general cure. The corpus has checked the popular one:

Contested

Household vinegar is an effective weed killer.

Vinegar only burns the tops of small, young weeds — it doesn't reach the roots, so established and perennial weeds grow right back, and it kills whatever it touches, including your crops. The stronger "horticultural" vinegar is caustic, not gentle. Use it for seedlings in pavement cracks, not as a lawn-and-bed weed killer.

what is actually known

Household vinegar (about 5% acetic acid) is a contact desiccant: sprayed on a sunny day it burns the top growth of young, small annual weeds. It does not move into the roots, so anything established or perennial regrows from the crown within a week or two, and it is not selective — it burns whatever it lands on, crop included. The stronger "horticultural" vinegar (20%+) is not a gentle alternative: it is a corrosive that can burn skin and eyes and needs real protective gear. For seedlings in cracks it is a fine spot treatment; for a real weed problem it is not the tool it is sold as.

what this rests on - one pointer

acetic acid is a non-selective contact desiccant that does not translocate to roots; horticultural vinegar is corrosive; extension weed-management guidance

Boiling water and flame weeders share vinegar's profile: real damage to small top growth, nothing to an established root, and no memory of the seed bank waiting underneath. As for herbicides proper - this site doesn't do spray recommendations, and around food crops the honest note is that even the common ones kill vegetables as readily as weeds; anyone who chooses that route is bound to the label, not to garden folklore. The mulch-and-hoe pair above is cheaper and it compounds.

The weeds you plant yourself

Some of the most stubborn weeds in a food garden arrived in a pot, on purpose, with a receipt. The corpus grades two of them:

Promising

Mint spreads aggressively by underground rhizomes and will colonize a bed, out-competing and crowding its neighbours if grown loose in open ground. Do not plant it directly in a shared bed. Contain it in a pot, or in a bottomless container sunk into the ground, and harvest and pull strays to keep it in bounds.

suboptimal if ignored → advise

Mechanism: Rhizomatous, and stoloniferous, spread. Mint's horizontal underground stems - rhizomes - and its surface runners root at their nodes and send up new shoots, so an unconfined plant expands outward from the crown each season and reappears wherever a node reaches soil. This vegetative creep is why a loose planting takes over a bed, and why a physical soil barrier, not a spacing rule, is the control.

Evidence: confirmed against 2 sources in the research-based literature.

the 2 sources

Univ. of Illinois Extension, 'Mint' (Herbs), extension.illinois.edu/herbs/mint (read 2026-08-22): 'They grow from 1-3 feet tall and aggressively spread by underground rhizomes'; 'Because mints have a prolific growth habit, they will become invasive in the garden'; 'It is highly suggested that mints be grown in containers above ground'; 'Other suggestions for containing mints include planting mints in large bottomless containers that are sunk into the garden.'

Utah State Univ. Extension, 'How to Grow Mint in Your Garden', extension.usu.edu/yardandgarden/research/mint-in-the-garden (read 2026-08-22): 'Mint spreads quickly in open garden areas and will out-compete most garden plants'; 'Mint is a rapid growing perennial herb with many varieties that grow up to 3 feet tall and are quite invasive'; 'Consider separate beds or grow mint in buried containers to contain the spreading rhizomes'; 'Containers should extend 3-4 inches above and 10-15 inches below the soil surface to ensure containment.'

remedy

Grow mint in a container above ground, or plant it in a large bottomless pot or buried edging sunk several inches into the bed - roughly 3-4 inches proud of the soil and 10-15 inches below - so escaping rhizomes are forced up over the lip where they show. Cut back and harvest through the season, and pull any runner that clears the barrier before it roots.

Promising

Lemon balm self-sows freely and, left to flower and set seed unchecked, spreads and can take over a bed. Cut back or deadhead the flowering stalks before they set seed to keep it in bounds, or grow it in a container. It is a strong self-seeder that can become weedy - though not a listed invasive.

suboptimal if ignored → advise

Mechanism: The spread is chiefly by self-sown seed: a plant left to flower sets abundant seed that drops and germinates in place, so it recurs and expands from year to year. Removing the flowering stalks before seed sets breaks that cycle. Its spread is by seed rather than by the running underground stems that carry its relative mint, though a soil-grown plant can also creep somewhat at its edges.

Evidence: confirmed against 2 sources in the research-based literature.

the 2 sources

Univ. of Illinois Extension, 'Lemon Balm', extension.illinois.edu/herbs/lemon-balm (read 2026-08-22): 'While mint can spread rapidly by underground stems, lemon balm spreads by setting lots of seed and can, if allowed to go unchecked start to overtake the garden'; 'To prevent spreading, prune flowering stalks before they have a chance to set seed'; "'Compacta' - A more compact form of lemon balm that is also sterile which means the flowers do not set viable seed."

Utah State Univ. Extension, 'How to Grow Lemon Balm in Your Garden', extension.usu.edu/yardandgarden/research/lemon-balm-in-the-garden (read 2026-08-22): 'Lemon balm, like mint spreads rapidly (becomes weedy) and can take over an herb bed'; 'Growing plants in containers helps control this problem'; 'If planted in the soil, harvest the leaves regularly, remove the flowers before they set seed, and dig around the plant edges to reduce root spreading.'

remedy

Prune or deadhead the flowering stalks before they set seed, and pull self-sown seedlings while young. Grow it in a container to contain it; for a soil-grown plant, cutting around the plant's edges checks the modest edge-creep. A sterile-flowered cultivar sets no viable seed and does not self-sow.

The pattern generalises: anything sold with the words "vigorous ground cover" deserves a container, a barrier, or a very deliberate decision. It's a far easier weed to prevent at planting time than to evict in year three - which is, come to think of it, this whole guide in one sentence.
