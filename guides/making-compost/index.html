Making compost - hot piles, cold piles, and what to leave out

How to make compost that actually finishes: what goes in, hot versus cold piles, the myths worth skipping, and the one contamination risk worth taking seriously.

What you need

  • A corner, bin or ring about 1 m3 feet each way - smaller piles won't heat
  • Browns - dry leaves, straw, shredded plain newspaper
  • Greens - vegetable scraps, coffee grounds, fresh clippings
  • A fork for turning, if you want the fast version

Compost is what you get when you pile up the garden's leftovers and let the decomposers do their work, and it's the cheapest soil improvement there is. Nine of the guides on this shelf tell you to add it. This one tells you how to make it, at either of the two honest speeds, and what to keep out of the pile so it stays a help rather than a hazard.

What compost actually is

A compost pile is a slow fire run by microorganisms. Feed them right and they'll burn through a heap of scraps and leaves in a season; starve them and the same heap just sits there, going quietly slimy.

Their diet has two halves. Browns are the dry, carbon-rich half: autumn leaves, straw, shredded plain newspaper, twigs thinner than a pencil. Greens are the moist, nitrogen-rich half: vegetable and fruit scraps, coffee grounds, fresh grass clippings, spent plants. Wisconsin Horticulture's rule of thumb is about two parts browns to one part green. You don't need to weigh anything - just notice what the pile is getting. All scraps and no leaves turns wet and smelly; all leaves and no scraps sits cold and still.

The other thing they need is water. A working pile should feel like a wrung-out sponge - damp all the way through, never dripping. In a dry spell, water it like anything else you're growing.

Hot or cold - two honest speeds

There are two ways to run a pile, and both of them end in compost. The difference is speed, and what the heat does along the way.

A hot pile is the managed version. Build it at least 1 m3 feet in each direction - smaller piles genuinely can't hold their heat - mix browns and greens, keep it damp, and the centre will climb to 54-71°C130-160°F within days. Turn it with a fork every week or two as the centre cools, moving the outside in, and you can have finished compost in two to five months. The heat is doing real work: held above 54°C130°F, it kills most weed seeds, insect eggs and plant diseases in the middle of the pile.

A cold pile is the patient version. Heap things up as they come, and walk away. No turning, no recipe. It works - decomposition doesn't need your permission - but it takes a year or more, and it never gets hot enough to kill anything. Weed seeds and diseases come through a cold pile alive, which is why the what-stays-out list below matters more if this is your speed. Most gardeners run something in between, and that's fine too.

What goes in, and what stays out

In: vegetable and fruit scraps, coffee grounds, eggshells, dead flowers and spent plants, autumn leaves, straw, shredded plain newspaper, small twigs. Grass clippings are good material but pack into airless mats on their own - mix them in with looser stuff.

Out, and for concrete reasons rather than fussiness:

  • Meat, bones, grease, dairy, whole eggs. They rot rather than compost, and they draw rodents and every other animal in the neighbourhood.
  • Pet waste. Dog and cat feces can carry diseases that pass to people, and a home pile can't reliably get hot enough to make them safe.
  • Badly diseased plants, and weeds gone to seed. A properly hot pile kills these; a cold or half-tended one doesn't. If you're not running a genuinely hot pile, send them off with the municipal yard waste instead - those piles run far hotter than yours.
  • Persistent perennial weeds. Quackgrass and bindweed can treat a cool pile as a propagation bed.

The myths, checked against the evidence

Compost attracts folklore the way it attracts worms. The good news is that most of the myths end the same way: the ingredient belongs in the pile, just not in the starring role the story gives it.

Contested

Compost tea (or manure tea) greatly boosts growth and suppresses plant disease.

Compost tea is a weak liquid feed at best — no better than using the compost itself, which also improves the soil the tea can't. The "cures disease" claims don't hold up in trials, and a badly brewed or manure-based tea can grow harmful bacteria you don't want on food crops. Put your compost on the beds; skip the tea as a disease cure.

what is actually known

Steeping compost in water extracts a little soluble nutrient, so a mild fertiliser effect is real — but no larger than just using the compost itself, which also builds the soil structure the tea does not. The bigger claim, that spraying compost tea protects plants from disease, is not reliably supported: trials are inconsistent and the effect, where seen at all, is small. There is a real downside, too: a poorly-aerated (anaerobic) brew, or one made with manure, can multiply human pathogens like E. coli and Salmonella, which is why food-safety guidance warns against spraying it on edible parts. Compost tea is a minor supplement at best, not a tonic or a fungicide.

what this rests on - one pointer

University of Connecticut, 'Compost, Compost Tea, and Manure: Food Safety Implications in the Vegetable Garden' (soiltesting.cahnr.uconn.edu/compost_compost_tea_and_manure, read 2026-07-14): 'ingredients commonly added to compost tea may promote growth of bacteria that can cause illness in humans, including Salmonella and E. coli'; unless methods minimize pathogens, 'it would be wise to save them ... for nonedible plants.' Backs the food-safety caveat; the disease-suppression benefit is inconsistent in trials.

Contested

Coffee grounds acidify soil.

Used coffee grounds are close to pH-neutral — brewing washes the acid out — so they won't durably acidify your soil or make it blueberry-friendly. Compost them or use a thin mulch for the organic matter; to lower pH, use elemental sulfur guided by a soil test.

what is actually known

Fresh grounds are mildly acidic, but brewing extracts most of the acids, so used (spent) grounds are near neutral and their effect on soil pH is small and inconsistent. They will not durably acidify soil to blueberry range. As a thin mulch or compost input they are fine — a modest slow-release nitrogen source. To actually lower pH, use elemental sulfur guided by a soil test.

what this rests on - one pointer

University of Minnesota Extension, 'Do common soil health home remedies work?' (extension.umn.edu/manage-soil-nutrients/coffee-grounds-eggshells-epsom-salts, read 2026-07-14): 'Coffee grounds have not been shown to consistently lower soil pH.' Used (spent) grounds are near-neutral, so whatever pH change they bring is small and short-lived.

Contested

Burying banana peels feeds plants potassium (roses in particular).

Banana peels do hold some potassium, but a buried fresh peel releases next to nothing until it rots — months away — and can draw pests in the meantime. Compost them instead; if a plant needs potassium, a soil test tells you, and a balanced amendment delivers it now.

what is actually known

A banana peel does contain potassium, but it is locked in undecomposed tissue. Buried whole and fresh it releases almost nothing on any useful timescale — it has to rot down first, which takes months — and meanwhile a shallow-buried peel can attract flies, rodents, and mould. The potassium is real; the "instant fertiliser" delivery is not. Composting it first turns the peel into a genuine slow contribution to organic matter.

what this rests on - one pointer

nutrients in undecomposed peel are not plant-available on a useful timescale; extension consensus

Contested

Crushed eggshells deter slugs.

Eggshells don't reliably deter slugs — they cross far sharper surfaces on their mucus layer, and the "barrier" weathers away fast. Use beer traps, night hand-picking, or iron-phosphate bait; put the shells in the compost for the calcium.

what is actually known

The claim is that sharp edges form a barrier slugs won't cross. Slugs glide on a mucus layer and cross genuinely sharp surfaces; crushed eggshell weathers to blunt fragments quickly and does not reliably stop them. The shells do add a little calcium as they break down — that is their real contribution, in the compost, not as a barrier.

what this rests on - one pointer

slug locomotion on mucus; barrier trials do not support

And one about what compost can't do - it feeds the ground you have, but it doesn't replace it:

Contested

If you buy good compost or bagged soil, your native ground underneath doesn't matter.

Buying good soil doesn't let you ignore the ground underneath. A thin layer over compacted native soil perches water at the boundary, and roots reach the native soil within a season anyway. Improve your own soil with organic matter over time; if drainage is truly bad, go with a tall raised bed or containers rather than a shallow cap of bagged mix.

what is actually known

Good compost or a bagged mix helps, but a thin cap of it over unimproved ground is not a fix, and in-ground it can make things worse. Water moves poorly across the sharp interface between a fluffy imported layer and compacted native soil below, so it perches at the boundary; and roots reach the native soil within a season, so its drainage and structure still govern how the plant does. Raised beds are more forgiving because the rooting volume is deeper, but they still interact with the ground at their base. The durable answer is to improve the native soil with organic matter over time — and where drainage is genuinely bad, a tall raised bed or containers with a consistent mix, not a shallow layer of good soil over bad.

what this rests on - one pointer

a texture interface between an imported layer and compacted native soil perches water and impedes root and water movement across the boundary; roots reach native soil within a season; extension consensus on soil improvement vs layering

The one real hazard: herbicide carryover

There's one way a batch of compost can genuinely wreck a garden, and it's worth knowing by name. A family of pasture and lawn herbicides - aminopyralid, clopyralid, picloram - passes intact through grazing animals and survives composting. Clopyralid can stay active in manure, hay or compost for several years, and composting contaminated material isn't a fix; as the pile shrinks, the concentration can actually rise.

Bring it in through treated lawn clippings, sprayed hay or straw, or manure from animals fed treated forage, and the damage shows up in exactly the crops a vegetable garden is full of: tomatoes, peppers, potatoes, peas and beans twist, cup their leaves, and stall.

The defence is boring and effective: know your inputs. Your own untreated clippings and leaves are safe by construction. For manure, straw or hay from elsewhere, ask what was sprayed on the pasture or the field - and if nobody can tell you, don't build your vegetable compost out of it.

When it's done, and how to use it

Finished compost doesn't look like what went in. It's dark brown, moist, smells like forest floor, and the ingredients have stopped being recognisable. If you can still tell what something was, give it longer.

Then use it generously. Work a layer about 5 cm2 inches deep into a vegetable bed before planting, or spread 5-8 cm2-3 inches around established plants as a mulch and let the worms do the incorporating. It's the same organic-matter medicine the soil test and last year's raised-bed soil guides keep prescribing - this is just the guide where you make the medicine yourself.
