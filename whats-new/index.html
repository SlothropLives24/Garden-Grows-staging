October 2026

  • Renaming a bed renames it

    Give a bed a new name in the Plan and it keeps its plans and its place. Read more

    Before, saving left a copy under the old name on the same spot, so the garden counted one bed too many.

  • The front page starts with your planting dates

    October 30, 2026 · The whole app

    Type your town or ZIP code on the front page and you go straight to your planting dates. Read more

    Further down, the evidence opens with a pair of watering answers: one the studies back, and one old tip they rule out.

    Try it ›

  • Keep a covered crop going after the first frost

    October 29, 2026 · Log

    When the Log offers to mark the tender crops ended by frost, each crop has its own tick. Read more

    Untick one you’re keeping going under a cover, and the same tap ends the rest. The next-season plan names a plant family the way the Plan does, the soil card writes every date the same way, and the bars on the coverage map’s cards are easier to tell apart.

    Try it ›

  • The Plan reads your bed’s rotation, and a season ends in fewer taps

    October 29, 2026 · Plan

    After a season of beans and squash, the Plan stops leading that bed with a team that brings them back. Read more

    Those teams come after the others, and a line names the plant families that grew there too recently. Once your first fall frost has passed, the Log marks the tender crops still in the ground as ended by frost in one tap, on the day you logged the frost, or else on your usual first frost date. When a bed’s plants come out on the same day, the Calendar shows one line for each crop. A screen reader now reaches each team card’s Use and Compare buttons on its own, and the Calendar’s days from the months on either side are easier to read.

    Try it ›

  • A nearer weather station, named where its frosts end sooner

    October 28, 2026 · This site

    In a few cities, Milpa plans frost dates by a frostier weather station farther out. Read more

    Where a station nearer the city records its last frost weeks sooner, or none at all, the city’s frost page, your climate card and Answers now name that station and its record, and your plans still go by the frostier one. The coverage map also shows, in a lighter shade, everywhere Milpa answers from the weather model, out to 160 km from its squares, and a country with a frost page now links straight to it, from the map and from the list of countries beneath it.

    Try it ›

  • The soil card reads more easily

    October 28, 2026 · Log

    On the soil card, the advice on finding a lab now gives each point its own line, and where it sends you to the international standard for testing labs it names it, ISO/IEC 17025, so you can ask a lab about it. Read more

    In the US it asks your lab for the pH in water too. The boxes for a lab report now have their names above them, and the privacy page says you can remove a soil reading and what stays behind. Weather stations abroad show their initials in capitals. In Spanish, the app now says frost comes in most winters, as the site does.

    Try it ›

  • Your season review plans for the right season

    October 28, 2026 · Log

    If a season stayed open past its year, the season review now plans for the coming one, as the Log does. Read more

    Plant teams you chose for a season that never started now move to the coming one too. A soil card that opened before your saved soil had loaded can no longer save its blank answers over it. And the app now clears out the old copies each update left on your phone, so it takes up less room.

    Try it ›

  • Home, the Log and the planner agree on next season

    October 28, 2026 · Log

    If a season stayed open past its year, Home and the planner could name one already over. Read more

    Now they name the coming season, as the Log does, and the plant teams you plan for it are there when you start it. A garden placed by a pin in Australia or the UK now gets its own country’s soil-test help. And a few smaller things: milpa.garden/web/#/home opens Home instead of a page saying it can’t be found, the Log’s filter row fades at its edge while more filters wait off-screen and no longer offers a bed with nothing in it, and on a phone the Edit ground button no longer sits on a bed’s name.

    Try it ›

    On a slow connection the climate card could say, for a moment, that your place had no hardiness zone and wasn’t covered, before your real frost dates arrived. Read more

    Now it says it’s reading your climate until they do.

  • Counts that add up, Spanish from the first screen, and a week that says one thing

    October 24, 2026 · The whole app

    A plant team’s plan now says how many plants its layout places; it used to count the kinds. Read more

    A phone set to Spanish opens Milpa in Spanish, with the switch to English on Home. The Calendar no longer lists a spring sowing as still open beside its fall sowing, a passed indoor start says so instead of rolling a year ahead, “Log the frost” lands on the Log’s frost card, and a raised bed’s depth is asked in your own units.

    Try it ›

  • Say what happened in one line, and the entry is filled in for you

    October 24, 2026 · Log

    Type or speak a plain sentence into the Log, like “frost last night”, and tap Log it. Read more

    It also reads “planted 12 tomatoes in bed 3” and “aphids on the kale”: the Log turns the line, on your phone, into the entry it means, a planting, a frost or a problem on a plant, and shows it back to you to check or change before anything is saved. The reading happens on your device: no service sees your words, and nothing is sent anywhere. A sentence it can’t read is kept as the note you wrote, so nothing is ever guessed. It works in English and Spanish, and with your keyboard’s voice typing.

    Try it ›

  • Where Milpa works: your ZIP code on the map

    October 24, 2026 · This site

    Type a US ZIP code into the map’s search and it shows the weather station that answers there. Read more

    The card names the station, how far it is and how sure that makes the dates, with a dashed line to it on the map; tap anywhere in the US for the same answer. Alaska, Hawaii and the territories are frames of their own now, a list names every place in view, and two cities on one spot both get their door.

    Try it ›

  • Find your way around the Log: by year, bed and kind

    October 23, 2026 · Log

    The Log’s diary now has a heading for each year, with how many entries it holds. Read more

    A line above it says which season you’re growing, which you’re planning and which are behind you, and each year in it takes you to that part of the diary. Every entry names its bed, or the whole garden, and what kind it is, and a row of buttons narrows the diary to one bed, one kind, or both. With more than one garden open, the diary, the editor and Undo say which garden an entry belongs to. A planting dated in the future is shown so you can fix the date or keep it.

    Try it ›

  • Every answer ends with a next step

    October 23, 2026 · This site

    Every answer in the app and every page on the site now ends with one clear next step and two more doors. Read more

    Each carries what you were looking at: a plant page asks when to plant it where you are, a frost page offers what can go in now, the symptom finder leads on to that crop’s planting date. The frost tools have one home, the ZIP-or-city lookup on the frost page’s first screen. And a plant Milpa warns against, like autumn olive, is never offered a planting date.

    Try it ›

  • Where Milpa works: a map you can use

    October 23, 2026 · This site

    The map on the page about where Milpa works is rebuilt. Read more

    The world is shaded by how many of each country’s cities read a weather station’s own frost dates, with no crowd of dots until you zoom in. Search for a country, a city or a US state, or tap a country, and the map glides there, outlines it in detail and names its cities. On a big screen the map can fill the window, the mouse wheel and the keyboard both work, and the address keeps the view so you can share it. The US shows its NOAA weather stations once you reach a state.

    Try it ›

  • The front page shows the whole season

    October 22, 2026 · The whole app

    Under its doors, the front page shows what Milpa works out for each part of the season. Read more

    Before you plant, it gives the frost odds for Minneapolis from a NOAA weather station. While it grows, it lists a week of jobs in a sample garden in Houston and answers one problem, blossom-end rot, with how sure the answer is. For keeping track, it shows lines from the example garden’s diary from this time last year. For a tip you’ve heard, it gives the tip of the day with its verdict and the source behind it. Each band ends at its own doors.

    Try it ›

  • Soil tests that fit your country, and a pH reading you can remove

    October 22, 2026 · Log

    A garden with an Australian or UK address now gets that country’s own route to a soil test. Read more

    The soil card names a lab accredited by NATA or UKAS and, in the UK, the RHS’s soil analysis service. In countries without a card of their own, it says how to find a lab you can trust and what its report should say about your pH, and when we read the pages behind that advice. A lime or sulfur rate printed on your lab report is the one to use, however your lab worked it out. A pH reading saved by mistake can be removed, with Undo. When the soil card can’t check a lab pH, Home and the Log ask for what it needs, which pH your lab gave or a pH in water. The card writes dates the way the rest of the app does, follows your units for the ribbon test and mound heights, and takes a report that grades nutrients on another scale or gives organic carbon instead of organic matter.

    Try it ›

  • Where Milpa works, made plain

    October 22, 2026 · This site

    The page on where Milpa works is short and plain. Read more

    One table shows each source of frost dates, where it reaches, how sure it is and how many places it covers, and one line sums up the rest of what Milpa knows. The map fills a phone’s width, outlines each country, shades the ground the weather model reads and draws the US as its NOAA weather stations. City pages abroad still say where their dates come from and how sure they are, in fewer words.
