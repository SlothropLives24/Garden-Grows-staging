Small, soft, pear-shaped insects clustered on a plant, with twisted and curled leaves, are aphids

Small, soft, pear-shaped insects clustered on a plant, with twisted and curled leaves, are aphids. They feed on the sap; heavy feeding yellows and stunts the plant.

Promising A person has checked it against its sources. If you skip this, it still grows, just less well. The planner suggests it.

How it works

Aphids feed on plant sap, injecting saliva into the host as they feed. Severe feeding twists and curls the leaves, yellows the foliage, stunts or kills shoots and slows growth. They secrete a sticky, sugary honeydew that can let sooty mold grow on the leaves, though the honeydew itself does not harm the plant.

What to do about it

Knock them off with a strong spray of water from the hose, which also washes off honeydew. Protect the lady beetles, lacewings, hover fly larvae and parasitic wasps that eat them. Avoid broad pesticides, and plant flowers that bloom at different times through the season. Insecticidal soap or horticultural oil can control aphids where needed.

How much it matters: No damage threshold is invented. The source ties the harm to SEVERE feeding (twisted leaves, yellowing, stunted shoots); a few aphids on a vigorous plant often need nothing, which is why the first remedy is a hose and the natural enemies. The rule advises and never blocks.

How we grade evidence

Well established
The rule names its mechanism and is settled, by sources a person has read and checked or by plain arithmetic and geometry.
Promising
The mechanism is named and the literature backs it. A few questions are still open.
A good hunch
Plausible by its mechanism, but field studies are thin, so treat it as a lean.
Contested
The evidence points both ways, or no study has tested it, so the planner does not enforce it.
Refuted
The claim did not survive its sources. It is kept so the folklore can be answered.
