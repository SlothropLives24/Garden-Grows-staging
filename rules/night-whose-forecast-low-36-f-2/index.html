Frost can form on a night the forecast low is a few degrees above freezing.

As recorded: On a night whose forecast low is 36 F (2 C) or colder, tender and very tender crops can be frosted, because the forecast is the air's temperature a couple of meters up and, on a clear, still night, the plants run colder. Half-hardy and hardy crops take a light freeze, and can be hurt at 28 F (-2 C) or colder.

Promising A person has checked it against its sources. If you skip this, the planting fails. The planner warns you.

How it works

On a clear, still night leaves lose heat to the open sky, so a leaf can fall to freezing and ice can form in it while the thermometer, a couple of meters up, still reads a few degrees above.

What to do about it

The evening before, cover the crops at risk with a sheet or frost cloth, never plastic, reaching the ground and loose at the bottom so the soil's warmth can rise under it, and take it off in the morning. A cover helps least in a long, hard freeze.

Where this rule stops

It reads the forecast low for a spot about 10 km across, so a garden in low ground, where cold air pools on still nights, can frost on a night the forecast says it won't: look to your low ground first. A forecast can be off by a few degrees, and it cannot say whether the night will be clear and still, so from 33 to 36 °F it says frost is possible, not certain. Guides sort crops into tiers differently: the tiers are Colorado State's, and South Dakota State puts some of that hardy tier (lettuce, spinach, peas) among the crops that take only a light freeze, so hardy crops share half-hardy's 28 °F line, though the cabbage family usually takes a few degrees more.

Crops in the ground, in open or roofed beds; a bed indoors is left out. The lines are the weather service's: a frost advisory for forecast lows of 33 to 36 °F, a freeze at 32 °F or under, and 28 °F, below which, its safety page says, a long freeze "can kill most types of commercial crops and residential plants". 28 °F is also where UMN's hard frost begins and South Dakota State's light freeze ends. It sets no date: the Calendar's frost dates stay on the normals and a forecast never moves them. The lines ride the trigger in Fahrenheit, as the sources give them, and are compared with the forecast's Celsius converted.
