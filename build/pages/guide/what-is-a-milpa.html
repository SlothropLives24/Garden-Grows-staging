Most people meet this idea as the “Three Sisters”: corn, beans, and squash grown together. The Three Sisters is the compact, three-crop version of something larger. The milpa is a whole cultivated field of interplanted crops that Mesoamerican farmers have grown for thousands of years, and the three sisters are its core. If you want the concept and the history, the explainer what a milpa is covers why the Three Sisters is only part of it; this guide is about how you’d grow one.

What does a milpa add beyond the three crops?

A milpa starts with the same engine (corn for structure, beans to climb it, squash to cover the ground) and then adds to it. Chiles, tomatoes, amaranth, and other regional crops share the same field, so a milpa is more a way of filling a whole plot with cooperating plants at different heights and habits than a fixed recipe. The Three Sisters mound is a single planting you can hold in your hands. The milpa is a system spread across a field, managed over a season and, traditionally, rotated and rested over years.

What does a milpa look like planted out?

Here’s the milpa as the planner lays it out, using the real placement geometry:

It’s organized as rows with paths between them, where a bed would be one solid block you reach across. That follows the rule that governs any planting big enough to walk into:

A planted bed keeps every plant within arm's reach of a path, edge, or walkable row.

Promising

If you skip this, it still grows, just less well. The planner suggests it.

How it works: You can comfortably reach about 2 feet (0.6 m) into a bed. Soil farther than that from a path or edge can only be tended by stepping on it, which packs it down. So a bed worked from its edges and wider than about 4 feet (1.2 m) is laid out as planting strips no wider than 4 feet, with paths about 20 inches (0.5 m) between them. A field is worked from rows you walk, about 2.5 feet (0.75 m) apart. Raised beds, in-ground beds and containers are worked from their edges; a field is walked.

A milpa is a walked field. You tend it from alleys between the rows, the way you can’t with a raised bed you reach into from the edge. At field scale, the access has to be inside the planting, which is why the layout above is rows and lanes.

How would I grow a small piece of one?

Few home gardeners have room for a true field-scale milpa, and that’s fine. The way in is to grow a piece of it. Start with a Three Sisters mound or a small block of them, keep the corn in a block so it pollinates, let the beans climb and the squash run, and then add a companion crop or two into the same ground. Scale up from there as you get room and confidence. The same cautions apply as for the Three Sisters. The beans structure the planting and improve the soil for next year, but they don’t fertilize this year’s corn; the do beans really feed the corn guide has that story.

How does a milpa work over several years?

Time is the last thing that makes a milpa a milpa. Traditionally the field is cropped and then rested or rotated, so the ground isn’t asked to grow the same families in the same place forever. That’s the long-run version of the logic behind crop rotation. Grown that way, a milpa is one of the oldest working examples of a polyculture that feeds people and maintains its own ground, and this planner is built to help you arrange exactly that kind of system.
