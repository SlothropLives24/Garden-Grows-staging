Plant your first bed, step by step

A first-season walkthrough. Pick a few forgiving crops, count from your frost dates, start what needs a head start, harden it off, and get it in the ground at the right time.

What you need

  • A bed with at least six to eight hours of sun a day
  • Your local frost dates (the planner gives them from your address)
  • A few beginner crops - a fast leaf crop, a large-seeded crop, one warm-season transplant
  • Seeds, and a sunny windowsill or a light for the transplant

Your first bed is easier than the internet makes it look. The trick is to plant a few forgiving crops and let your frost dates tell you when. Here’s the whole sequence on a small bed, with three crops that between them teach the three ways a plant starts: a fast leaf crop you scatter now, a big-seeded crop you push straight into the ground, and one warm-season crop you give a head start indoors.

Where do I start - the spot and the crops?

Choose a bed in full sun (six to eight hours) that you can reach without stepping on. Then pick three easy crops: radish or lettuce (a fast cool-season crop you sow direct), bush beans (a big seed that goes straight in the ground), and one tomato (a warm-season crop worth starting early indoors). Don’t crowd them. Every plant needs its mature spread, and the planner will hold you to it:

How do I use my frost date to plan?

Everything from here is timed off your last spring frost. Get it from the planner (it comes with your address), and remember it’s an average. Some years frost comes later. The frost dates guide explains how to read the odds. You’ll count backward from it for the tomato and forward for the beans.

When do I start the tomato indoors?

A tomato needs a running start to ripen in a normal season, so it goes on a windowsill or under a light several weeks before it can move outside. The planner schedules the exact head start:

Long-season warm crops are started indoors several weeks before the last frost and set out after it. Tomato and tomatillo go about six weeks ahead, pepper and eggplant about eight, basil six.

Promising

If you skip this, it still grows, just less well. The planner suggests it.

How it works: A frost-tender crop with a long days-to-maturity cannot mature if it is only direct-sown after the last frost, because the frost-free window is too short. A head start under cover buys the weeks the season lacks; the seedling is set out only once frost has passed, so the transplant date is still frost-gated.

When can I sow the cool-season crop?

Radish and lettuce are hardy. They want the cool. Sow them straight into the bed about three weeks before your last frost, radish 1.3 cm0.5 in deep and lettuce 0.6 cm0.2 in, and thin the seedlings to their spacing once they’re an inch or two tall (snip them; pulling disturbs the neighbors). This is the hardy-crop offset in action:

Cold-hardy annuals can be sown or transplanted before the last spring frost (hardy crops about three weeks before, half-hardy about two weeks before). Tender crops wait for the frost itself.

Promising

If you skip this, it still grows, just less well. The planner suggests it.

How it works: Cold hardiness. Hardy crops tolerate hard frost and half-hardy crops tolerate light frost, so they can occupy the ground weeks before frost-tender crops are allowed in. The offset is measured from the last spring frost.

When do the beans and the tomato go out?

Once the danger of frost has passed and the soil has warmed, push the bean seeds an inch into the ground where they’ll grow. Big-seeded crops resent transplanting and catch up fine from a direct sow. The tomato goes out about two weeks later, once the days stay warm, because it’s very tender and cool days stunt it. Plant it deeper than it grew in its pot, buried up to its first leaves, because a tomato grows roots all along a buried stem. Water everything in.

How do I keep the leaf crop producing?

Radish and lettuce are fast and finish all at once, so re-sow a short row of radish every 7–10 days and one of lettuce every 10–14 days. That’s succession sowing, and it turns one glut into a steady harvest:

Succession sowing at intervals of about two weeks extends harvest for short-DTM crops.

Promising

If you skip this, it still grows, just less well. The planner suggests it.

How it works: Sowing the same crop in batches about two weeks apart staggers when each batch matures, so the harvest arrives as a continual succession, with no single glut. It pays off only for short-DTM crops, whose quick turnaround lets several sowings ripen within a single season.

That’s a garden

Sun, the right timing off your frost dates, spacing the plants can live with, and a habit of re-sowing the fast crop. Log what you planted and where. Next year that record is what lets the crop rotation move each family to fresh ground.
