What a milpa is, and why Three Sisters is only part of it

The best-known polyculture in North American gardening is really the northern tip of a much older field system, and the difference comes down to scale.

The famous version

Corn, beans, and squash, planted together in a mound. The corn stands up, the beans climb it, the beans feed the soil, and the squash sprawls across the ground to shade out the weeds. Three Sisters is the one plant team just about every gardener has heard of, and unlike most of what gets called companion planting, it holds up. Every one of those relationships has a real reason behind it, one you can name and check.

It’s also a piece of something bigger. Three Sisters is the northern tip of the Mesoamerican milpa, and a milpa is a field.

The system it came from

The milpa is a whole field system with a fallow cycle, meaning a patch left to rest on purpose. It carries the same three roles and adds more: a fruiting layer of chile or tomatillo underneath, and a leaf crop like amaranth. It’s grown at field scale, and the rest year is part of the design.

  • The milpa as this corpus models it

The corpus puts a hard floor of 50 square meters540 square feet on it, and the reason is worth quoting:

Milpa is a field system with a fallow cycle, not a bed. Below field scale you are growing Three Sisters and calling it something else.

That keeps the word from drifting. You can grow four species in two square meters20 square feet and it might be a lovely planting, but calling it a milpa would make the word mean nothing.

Why the corn is the part that bites

The part of this system most likely to let a first-time grower down is the corn, and the reason is pollination, meaning how the pollen gets where it needs to go.

Corn needs a block of at least three short rows side by side for good kernel set, and four rows is better.

Well established

If you skip this, the planting fails. The planner won’t allow it.

How it works: Maize is wind-pollinated and protandrous (each plant sheds its pollen before its own silks are ready). Tassels shed pollen that must land on the silks of neighboring plants. In a single row much of the pollen blows out of the row, and each silk it misses is one missing kernel.

The claim that’s weaker than you’ve been told

The nitrogen story is the one everybody repeats: the beans feed the corn. It’s the shakiest of the three relationships, and the corpus grades it that way.

Beans fix nitrogen that feeds the corn growing beside them, that season.

A good hunch

This claim only partly holds up. The planner won’t plan around this claim. What is known instead is below.

What is known: Rhizobia in legume root nodules do fix atmospheric N. Most of it is incorporated into the legume's own tissue. Some does move to a neighboring non-legume the same season via direct root contact and rhizodeposition, but the amount is small (a few percent of the neighbor's N uptake) and does not meet a heavy feeder's demand. The bulk of the benefit arrives the following season, after residues decompose.

Most of the nitrogen a bean plant pulls from the air only becomes available once its roots and leaves break down, which is next season. Some transfer does happen between living plants, but the measured amounts are small. The beans do improve that ground. They just don’t fertilize this year’s corn in any amount that will change your harvest.

Two of the three relationships among the Three Sisters are strong. This one is a good hunch with real numbers attached, and it’s shown that way instead of being smoothed into the story.

What the squash actually does

Living mulch suppresses weeds and conserves soil moisture.

A good hunch

If you skip this, it still grows, just less well. The planner suggests it.

How it works: A living cover keeps a leaf canopy over the ground. It intercepts light at the soil surface, so weed seedlings are shaded out before they establish, and it slows surface evaporation, so the soil holds more water. In a Three Sisters planting this is the squash's role. Its broad leaves shade the bed.

This one holds up. The leaves cover the soil as a living mulch, the effect is measurable, and it earns the third sister her spot on her own. Scaring off pests has nothing to do with it.

Growing it at garden scale

If you’ve got a bed and no field, grow the Three Sisters and call it that. Give the corn a block of several short rows. Expect the beans to improve the ground for next year, with little effect on this year’s corn. And let the squash do the job it’s good at.

That’s the version of this system that survives a checked source, and it’s still a really good planting.
