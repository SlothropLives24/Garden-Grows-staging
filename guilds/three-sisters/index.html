Three Sisters

Corn, climbing beans and squash in one bed, the corn holding up the beans while the squash shades the soil below.

Space it needs

At least 4 m²43 ft². If your bed is smaller, the planner grays this plant team out, says why, and offers the nearest adaptation that fits. It never swaps in something else without telling you.

If your bed is smaller: Three Sisters (small bed) (1.5 m²16 ft²).

Variations on it: Four Sisters (4 m²43 ft²).

What it looks like planted

Between seasons

Nothing is left living in the bed over winter; the whole planting is re-established.

What it claims

Corn provides a living trellis

Well established

Squash foliage suppresses weeds and retains soil moisture

A good hunch

How it works: A living cover keeps a leaf canopy over the ground. It intercepts light at the soil surface, so weed seedlings are shaded out before they establish, and it slows surface evaporation, so the soil holds more water. In a Three Sisters planting this is the squash's role. Its broad leaves shade the bed.

The corn shades the squash fruit from strong summer sun once the squash plants lose their leaves

A good hunch

How it works: Sun scald. As a squash plant ages its leaves die back and leave the fruit uncovered, and strong summer sun burns the exposed rind. Corn standing in the same bed casts shade over that fruit.

Who does what, and the swaps

Each job in this planting can be done by more than one plant. The usual choice comes first, and each swap says what it costs.

Support

  • Usual choice: Corn
  • Swaps: Sunflower
  • What a swap costs: Sunflower can carry the beans, but it is a heavy nitrogen feeder that competes with the beans and casts shade, and it needs a ~30-day head start or the vines pull it down.

Fixer

  • Usual choice: Pole bean
  • Swaps: Runner bean, Cowpea, Pole lima

Cover

  • Usual choice: Vining squash
  • Swaps: Butternut, Winter squash, Sweet potato, Vining cucumber

A belief this raises

Companion planting charts encode real relationships.

Refuted

Most companion-planting advice you'll find online is folklore, and the charts have no scientific basis. A few pairings do have real mechanisms. Here's what we can actually defend, and why.

What is known

Companion-planting charts are not a record of tested relationships. The idea is old. Plants were once matched by the four elements and the signs of the zodiac, and later by pseudoscientific methods such as sensitive crystallization of plant extracts, and there is no scientific basis for any of the lists. A minority of pairings do have real, named mechanisms: mixed plantings confuse pests hunting for their host plant by sight and smell, a squash canopy works as a living mulch, and diverse plantings keep beneficial predators and parasitoids around. Those are recorded here as a good hunch. Most chart entries have nothing behind them.

Rules that decide where it fits

Corn needs a block of at least three short rows side by side for good kernel set, and four rows is better.

Well established

If you skip this, the planting fails. The planner won’t allow it.

How it works: Maize is wind-pollinated and protandrous (each plant sheds its pollen before its own silks are ready). Tassels shed pollen that must land on the silks of neighboring plants. In a single row much of the pollen blows out of the row, and each silk it misses is one missing kernel.

richmond.ces.ncsu.edu Read by a person on October 3, 2026

Glass Gem Corn NC State Extension, Richmond County Center
When tassels start dropping pollen, stalks can be gently shaken. This mimics wind and will release the pollen to float down to the silks.
Remedy

Plant corn in a block of several short rows rather than one long row; four rows is best. For a small patch, shake the stalks gently while the tassels shed pollen, or dust a cut tassel over the silks, every few days. Or leave corn out and give the beans a trellis.
