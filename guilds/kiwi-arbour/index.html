What to keep in mind

The vine is dioecious, so this arbor holds two vines, a male and a female of the same species, and a lone vine or two females sets no fruit; one male serves up to six females if you plant more. It is far heavier than a grapevine, up to 40 lb of fruit on a vine that can grow 30 ft in a year, so the structure is a stout arbor or T-bar on 4 to 6 inch posts. A grape trellis won't hold it. The understory stays a bulb ring at the posts because the canopy casts shade.

Don't plant anything sensitive to walnut toxin (juglone) within a walnut tree's root zone. Nightshades are sensitive, and so are apple and cabbage.

Well established

If you skip this, the planting fails. The planner won’t allow it.

How it works: Juglans roots exude hydrojuglone, oxidized to juglone, a respiration inhibitor. The nightshades are highly sensitive; they wilt and die.

Remedy

Plant it outside the walnut's root zone, which takes in the whole drip line and beyond. There is no soil treatment for juglone.

Some plants come as separate male and female plants, and you need one of each for fruit.

Well established

If you skip this, you lose real yield. The planner warns you.

How it works: A dioecious species carries male and female flowers on separate plants. Some plants make only pollen, others only fruit. A lone plant, or a planting that is all one sex, sets no fruit at all, because there is no pollen to reach the female flowers. Fruit needs at least one male plant near the fruiting females. This is a different mechanism from the single-locus self-incompatibility of apple and pear. There a plant has both sexes and rejects only its own pollen; here whole plants are one sex. So it is a separate rule and does not widen that one.

Evidence: checked against 1 source in the research-based literature.

The source

plants.ces.ncsu.edu Read by a person on September 1, 2026

Actinidia arguta NC State Extension Gardener Plant Toolbox
A male and female plant is needed for fruit production
Remedy

Plant at least one male alongside the female fruiting plant(s). A single plant, or several of the same sex, will never fruit. A few named cultivars are self-fertile and need no separate male; if you grow one of those, one plant is enough.
