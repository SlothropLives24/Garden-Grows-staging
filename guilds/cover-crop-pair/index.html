Winter cover pair

Winter rye and hairy vetch, sown together in early fall to cover bare ground over winter and cut down in spring.

Why these are grouped

A rest for the soil. It benefits next season’s crop as a rotation break and green manure. The plants are not claimed to help each other while they grow.

Plan this plant team ›

Space it needs

At least 0.3 m²3 ft². If your bed is smaller, the planner grays this plant team out, says why, and offers the nearest adaptation that fits. It never swaps in something else without telling you.

What it looks like planted

Between seasons

Nothing is left living in the bed over winter; the whole planting is re-established.

What it claims

The hardy grain stands up in spring and carries the climbing vetch

Well established

The legume's residue mineralizes N for the next crop

Promising

How it works: A legume fixes atmospheric nitrogen into its tissue as it grows. When the crop is killed and its residue decomposes, soil microbes mineralize that organic nitrogen, converting a form plants can't use into one they can, and release it for the next crop. How much depends on how well the legume grew, and it is lower on sandy soils.

Evidence: checked against 2 sources in the research-based literature.

When cover crops are killed and begin to decompose, nitrogen can be released from the residues back into the soil where it can feed the next crop

The nitrogen credit depends on the amount of crop growth and when the legume was planted. Nitrogen credits are 50 lb N/a lower on sandy, coarse-textured soils.

The grain takes up the soil nitrogen the legume does not need, holding it against winter leaching

Promising

The grain's residue suppresses small-seeded weeds, and the next small-seeded sowing with them

Promising

How it works: Rye and sorghum roots and residue release allelochemicals (benzoxazinoids from rye, sorgoleone from sorghum) that persist in the surface soil for weeks after the cover is killed. A small seed sown into that layer has little reserve to push through the inhibition; a large seed or a transplant is already past the germination window.

What to keep in mind

Two plants doing two jobs on ground that would otherwise sit bare. Rye is the grain that stands the winter; oats die back to a mat at the first hard frost, which is the swap for an early sowing or anywhere spring rye gets away from you. Crimson clover is the shorter legume that does not climb. Either swap gives up the climbing line, which only vetch on rye earns. Cut the rye at least two weeks before you sow anything small-seeded; the plan flags this for you. Hairy vetch is listed as invasive in Oregon and is weedy anywhere it sets seed, so cut it before it does. Buckwheat is the summer cover, grown on its own, and is not part of this pair.

Who does what, and the swaps

Each job in this planting can be done by more than one plant. The usual choice comes first, and each swap says what it costs.

Grain

  • Usual choice: Cereal rye
  • Swaps: Oats

Legume

  • Usual choice: Hairy vetch
  • Swaps: Crimson clover

What it costs the ground

Rotation history belongs to the ground itself, so this is what the planner records about this patch after the plant team comes out.

  • Legumes: counts as a full season of this family
  • Grasses: counts as a full season of this family

Where this plant team comes from

The grain-and-legume winter cover the extension cover-crop guides all describe (NC State 'Winter Annual Cover Crops'; UMass 'Cover Crop, Hairy Vetch'; Penn State 'Making the Most of Mixtures'; Clemson HGIC 'Cover Crops'; Univ. of Maryland 'Cover Crops for Gardens'), sown as a pair in early autumn and cut in spring.
