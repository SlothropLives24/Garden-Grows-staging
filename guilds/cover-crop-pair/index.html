Winter Cover Pair

A restorative break - planted to give the ground back what a rotation took.

The grain-and-legume winter cover the extension cover-crop guides all describe (NC State 'Winter Annual Cover Crops'; UMass 'Cover Crop, Hairy Vetch'; Penn State 'Making the Most of Mixtures'; Clemson HGIC 'Cover Crops'; Univ. of Maryland 'Cover Crops for Gardens' - all five fetched and read for this team), sown as a pair in early autumn and cut in spring.

What binds this planting

A rest for the soil. Its benefit is to next season's crop — a rotation break and green manure — not a synergy among plants grown together.

What it looks like planted

Between seasons

Nothing is left living in the bed over winter; the whole planting is re-established.

What it claims

the hardy grain stands up in spring and carries the climbing vetch

Well established

the legume's residue mineralizes N for the next crop

Promising

Mechanism: A legume fixes atmospheric nitrogen into its tissue as it grows. When the crop is killed and its residue decomposes, soil microbes mineralize that organic nitrogen — converting a form plants can't use into one they can — and release it for the next crop. How much depends on how well the legume grew, and it is lower on sandy soils.

Evidence: confirmed against 2 sources in the research-based literature.

the 2 sources

Penn State Extension, 'Growing Cover Crops for Nitrogen on Vegetable Farms', extension.psu.edu/growing-cover-crops-for-nitrogen-on-vegetable-farms (read 2026-07-11): 'When cover crops are killed and begin to decompose, nitrogen can be released from the residues back into the soil where it can feed the next crop'; 'Mineralization is the process where organic nitrogen, which is largely not available to plants, is converted by soil microorganisms' into a plant-available form.

Univ. of Wisconsin-Madison Extension (Crops & Soils), 'Late Summer Planting Legumes to Produce Nitrogen Credits for Next Year', cropsandsoils.extension.wisc.edu/articles/late-summer-planting-legumes-to-produce-nitrogen-credits-for-next-year (read 2026-07-11): 'The nitrogen credit depends on the amount of crop growth and when the legume was planted. Nitrogen credits are 50 lb N/a lower on sandy, coarse-textured soils.'

the grain takes up the soil nitrogen the legume does not need, holding it against winter leaching

Promising

the grain's residue suppresses small-seeded weeds, and the next small-seeded sowing with them

Promising

Mechanism: Rye and sorghum roots and residue release allelochemicals - benzoxazinoids from rye, sorgoleone from sorghum - that persist in the surface soil for weeks after the cover is killed. A small seed sown into that layer has little reserve to push through the inhibition; a large seed or a transplant is already past the germination window.

The honest version

Two plants doing two jobs on ground that would otherwise sit bare. Rye is the grain that stands the winter; oats die back to a mat at the first hard frost, which is the swap for an early sowing or anywhere spring rye gets away from you. Crimson clover is the shorter legume that does not climb. Either swap gives up the climbing line - only vetch on rye earns it. Cut the rye at least two weeks before you sow anything small-seeded; the plan flags this for you. Hairy vetch is listed as invasive in Oregon and is weedy anywhere it sets seed, so cut it before it does. Buckwheat is the summer cover, grown on its own, and is not part of this pair.
